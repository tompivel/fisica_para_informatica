### Torque y momento magnético de current loop (magnetic dipole)
**Magnitude of torque on a current loop:**

$$\tau = IBA \sin\phi \quad (27.23)$$

$$ \tau = \mu B \sin\phi \quad (27.25) $$

**Magnetic dipole moment:**

$$ \mu = IA \quad (27.24) $$

**Vector torque on a current loop:**

$$ \vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu} \times \vec{B} \quad (27.26) $$

$$U = -\vec{\mu} \cdot \vec{B} = -\mu B \cos\phi \quad \text{(potential energy for a magnetic dipole)} \quad (27.27)$$
**Explanation**

This equation defines the potential energy ($U$) of a magnetic dipole when placed in a magnetic field, derived from the mathematical symmetry with electric dipole interactions ($U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E}$).

- **$\vec{\mu}$** is the magnetic dipole moment vector, with magnitude $\mu$.
- **$\vec{B}$** is the magnetic field vector, with magnitude $B$.
- **$\phi$** is the angle between the magnetic dipole moment ($\vec{\mu}$) and the magnetic field ($\vec{B}$).

#### **Key Orientations and Energy States**
- **Stable Equilibrium ($\phi = 0^\circ$):** When the magnetic dipole is aligned parallel to the magnetic field, $\cos(0^\circ) = 1$. The potential energy is at its **minimum** ($U = -\mu B$).
- **Zero Energy State ($\phi = 90^\circ$):** When the dipole moment is oriented perpendicular to the field, $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$, resulting in zero potential energy ($U = 0$).
- **Unstable Equilibrium ($\phi = 180^\circ$):** When the dipole is oriented antiparallel (directly opposite) to the magnetic field, $\cos(180^\circ) = -1$. The potential energy is at its **maximum** ($U = +\mu B$).
### Explanation

- Equation (27.23) calculates the magnitude of the torque ($\tau$) on a current loop, where $I$ is the current, $A$ is the area of the loop, $B$ is the magnetic field magnitude, and $\phi$ is the angle between the magnetic field $\vec{B}$ and the normal to the loop (the vector area $\vec{A}$).
- Equation (27.24) defines the magnitude of the magnetic dipole moment ($\mu$) as the product of the current ($I$) and the area ($A$) of the loop.
- Equation (27.25) substitutes the magnetic moment into the torque equation, showing that the torque magnitude depends on the magnetic moment, the magnetic field, and the angle between them.
- Equation (27.26) provides the vector form of the interaction, showing that the torque vector ($\vec{\tau}$) is the cross product of the vector magnetic moment ($\vec{\mu}$) and the magnetic field vector ($\vec{B}$).

## Magnetic field of a circular current loop
![Pasted image 20260702125620](../assets/Pasted%20image%2020260702125620.png)
###  Magnetic Field on the Axis of a Circular Loop

$$B_x = \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2(x^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \quad (28.15)$$

**Explanation:**

This formula calculates the magnitude of the magnetic field ($B_x$) at a distance $x$ along the central axis perpendicular to a single circular current-carrying loop.

- $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space ($4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$).
- $I$ is the electric current flowing through the loop.
- $a$ is the radius of the circular loop.
- $x$ is the distance along the axis from the center of the loop to the field point.

The direction of this magnetic field is determined by the **right-hand rule**: curling the fingers of your right hand around the loop in the direction of the current points your right thumb in the direction of the magnetic field vector $\vec{B}$.

### Magnetic Field at the Center of $N$ Circular Loops

$$B_x = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2a} \quad (28.17)$$

**Explanation:**

This equation simplifies Equation (28.15) for the specific case where you evaluate the magnetic field directly at the center of the loop ($x = 0$), and extends it to a flat coil consisting of $N$ tightly wound identical loops.

- $N$ represents the number of turns (loops) in the coil.
- At the center ($x = 0$), the denominator simplifies from $2(0^2 + a^2)^{3/2}$ to $2a^3$, which reduces to $2a$ after canceling $a^2$ with the numerator.
- The field strength is at its maximum at the center ($x = 0$) and decreases in magnitude as you move outward along the axis.

### **What is a Magnetic Dipole in This Context?**

In this context, a **magnetic dipole** refers to the current-carrying circular loop itself when observed from a distance far away along its axis ($x \gg a$).

When the distance $x$ is much greater than the loop's radius $a$, the term $(x^2 + a^2)^{3/2}$ in Equation (28.15) can be approximated as $x^3$. The formula then simplifies to:

$$B_x \approx \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2x^3}$$

By multiplying the numerator and denominator by $\pi$, we introduce the area of the circular loop ($A = \pi a^2$) and define the **magnetic dipole moment** ($\mu = IA$):

$$B_x \approx \frac{\mu_0 (I \pi a^2)}{2\pi x^3} = \frac{\mu_0 \mu}{2\pi x^3}$$

At large distances, the current loop generates a magnetic field mathematically identical in form to the electric field generated by an electric dipole along its axis. Any closed current loop that produces this characteristic spatial field profile is defined as a magnetic dipole.
## Magnetic Materials

### Permeabilidad del Material ($28.30$)

$$\mu = K_m \mu_0$$

- **$\mu$**: Permeabilidad magnética del material.
- **$K_m$**: Permeabilidad relativa (adimensional).
- **$\mu_0$**: Permeabilidad del vacío ($4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$).
    
- **Aplicación**: En cualquier ecuación que relacione campos magnéticos con sus fuentes en el vacío, al sumergir un conductor en un material paramagnético (o magnético lineal), se reemplaza $\mu_0$ por el producto $K_m \mu_0$.

### Susceptibilidad Magnética ($28.31$)

$$\chi_m = K_m - 1$$

- **$\chi_m$**: Susceptibilidad magnética (adimensional). Representa la cantidad en la que la permeabilidad relativa $K_m$ difiere de la unidad ($1$).

### Diamagnetismo

- **Momento magnético inicial nulo**: En los materiales diamagnéticos, el momento magnético total de todas las espiras de corriente atómicas es cero en ausencia de un campo magnético externo.
    
- **Inducción del campo opuesto**: Al aplicar un campo externo, se modifica el movimiento orbital de los electrones en los átomos, induciendo dipolos magnéticos adicionales (explicado por la ley de inducción de Faraday / Ley de Lenz). El campo magnético adicional generado por estas corrientes inducidas se opone **siempre** a la dirección del campo externo aplicado.
    
- **Propiedades características**:
    - **Susceptibilidad negativa**: $\chi_m < 0$.
    - **Permeabilidad relativa menor a la unidad**: $K_m < 1$ (típicamente del orden de $0.99990$ a $0.99999$ para sólidos y líquidos).
    - **Independencia térmica**: A diferencia del paramagnetismo, la susceptibilidad diamagnética es virtualmente independiente de la temperatura.
	## Ferromagnetismo y Dominios Magnéticos

### Ferromagnetismo: Conceptos Fundamentales

En los materiales **ferromagnéticos** (como hierro, níquel, cobalto y sus aleaciones), existen interacciones mecánico-cuánticas extremadamente fuertes entre los momentos magnéticos atómicos. Esto provoca que se alineen paralelamente entre sí en pequeñas regiones llamadas **dominios magnéticos**, incluso en ausencia de un campo magnético externo.

#### Comportamiento frente a un Campo Externo ($\vec{B}_0$)

El mecanismo físico básico es idéntico al del paramagnetismo: **los momentos dipolares magnéticos tienden a alinearse en paralelo con el campo externo para minimizar su energía potencial**. Sin embargo, en el ferromagnetismo el proceso ocurre a escala macroscópica mediante el desplazamiento de las fronteras de los dominios:

- **Sin campo aplicado ($\vec{B}_0 = 0$):** Las magnetizaciones de los distintos dominios están orientadas al azar, por lo que el momento magnético total del material es nulo.
- **Con campo aplicado ($\vec{B}_0 > 0$):** Los dominios cuya orientación coincide con la dirección de $\vec{B}_0$ **crecen** desplazando sus fronteras, mientras que los dominios con otras orientaciones se encogen y rotan hacia la dirección del campo.
    

### Comparación Clave: Ferromagnetismo vs. Paramagnetismo

| **Propiedad**                      | **Ferromagnetismo (ej. Hierro)**                                                 | **Paramagnetismo (ej. Aluminio)**                                                |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mecanismo de alineación**        | Alineación **paralela** al campo $\vec{B}_0$ (mediante crecimiento de dominios). | Alineación **paralela** al campo $\vec{B}_0$ (átomos individuales).              |
| **Permeabilidad Relativa ($K_m$)** | **Extremadamente alta**<br><br>  <br><br>$$K_m \approx 10^3 \text{ a } 10^5$$    | **Ligeramente mayor a 1**<br><br>  <br><br>$$K_m \approx 1.000022$$              |
| **Intensidad de atracción**        | **Fuerte:** Los torques sobre dominios enteros son órdenes de magnitud mayores.  | **Débil:** La agitación térmica desorganiza fácilmente los dipolos individuales. |

> **Nota:** Aunque en ambos fenómenos los momentos magnéticos se alinean en paralelo al campo externo, la diferencia en magnitud es abismal. Debido a que $K_m$ en los materiales ferromagnéticos es de $1000$ a $100000$ veces mayor que en el vacío (mientras que en los paramagnéticos apenas supera la unidad por una fracción de $10^{-5}$), un imán común atrae un clavo de hierro con gran fuerza, pero es incapaz de levantar una lata de aluminio.

### Saturación y Histeresis

- **Saturación Magnética ($M_{\text{sat}}$):** A medida que se incrementa el campo externo $\vec{B}_0$, se alcanza un punto en el que prácticamente todos los momentos magnéticos del material quedan alineados en paralelo al campo. A partir de este límite, un aumento en $\vec{B}_0$ ya no incrementa la magnetización del material ($M$). En este régimen, $K_m$ deja de ser constante y decrece al aumentar el campo.
- **Histeresis:** Al reducir el campo externo de vuelta a cero tras haber alcanzado la saturación, el material retiene gran parte de su alineación (magnetización remanente). Este retraso en la desmagnetización se conoce como **histeresis**, y es el principio físico detrás de la creación de los **imanes permanentes**.
# Inductancia magnética
If the magnetic flux through a circuit changes, an emf and a current are induced in the circuit. 

Electromagnetic induction tells us that a time-varying magnetic field can act as a source of electric field.
### Flujo magnético
**Magnetic flux through element of area $d\vec{\boldsymbol{A}}$:**

$$d\Phi_B = \vec{\boldsymbol{B}} \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{A}} = B_{\perp} dA = B \, dA \cos\phi$$
**Total magnetic flux through a surface:**

$$\Phi_B = \int \vec{\boldsymbol{B}} \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{A}} = \int B_{\perp} dA = \int B \cos\phi \, dA$$
![Pasted image 20260624110932](../assets/Pasted%20image%2020260624110932.png)
### Ley de Faraday
 Faraday's law states that the induced emf in a closed loop equals the negative of the time rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop. This relationship is valid whether the flux change is caused by a changing magnetic field, motion of the loop, or both. (See Examples 29.1–29.6.)

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$
If we have a coil with N identical turns, and if the flux varies at the same rate $d \Phi_{B}$ through each turn, the total rate of change through all the turns is N times as large as for a single turn.
$$
\begin{align}

\mathcal{E} &  = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}  \\
\mathcal{E} &  = IR
\end{align}
$$

$$\begin{align} \mathcal{E} = \oint (\vec{\boldsymbol{v}} \times \vec{\boldsymbol{B}}) \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{l}} \qquad \text{(motional emf; closed conducting loop)} \end{align}$$
### Ley de Lenz 
says that any induction effect tends to oppose the change that caused it
### **Displacement current and Maxwell's equations**
A time-varying electric field generates a displacement current $i_{\text{D}}$, which acts as a source of magnetic field in exactly the same way as conduction current. The relationships between electric and magnetic fields and their sources can be stated compactly in four equations, called Maxwell's equations. Together they form a complete basis for the relationship of $\vec{\boldsymbol{E}}$ and $\vec{\boldsymbol{B}}$ fields to their sources.

$$i_{\text{D}} = \epsilon \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \tag{29.14}$$

(displacement current)

$$\oint \vec{\boldsymbol{E}} \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{A}} = \frac{Q_{\text{encl}}}{\epsilon_0} \tag{29.18}$$

(Gauss's law for $\vec{\boldsymbol{E}}$ fields)

$$\oint \vec{\boldsymbol{B}} \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{A}} = 0 \tag{29.19}$$

(Gauss's law for $\vec{\boldsymbol{B}}$ fields)

$$\oint \vec{\boldsymbol{B}} \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{l}} = \mu_0 \left( i_{\text{C}} + \epsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \right)_{\text{encl}} \tag{29.20}$$

(Ampere's law including displacement current)

$$\oint \vec{\boldsymbol{E}} \cdot d\vec{\boldsymbol{l}} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} $$

(Faraday's law)

## Self-Inductance
any circuit that carries a varying current has an emf induced in it by the variation in its own magnetic field. Such an emf is called a self-induced emf.
$$\begin{align} L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{i} \end{align}$$
donde i es la corriente que circula por el circuito. N es la cantidad de vueltas de la bobina.
$$\begin{align} \mathcal{E} = -L \frac{di}{dt} \qquad \text{(self-induced emf)} \end{align}$$
Inductors are among the indispensable circuit elements of modern electronics. Their purpose is to oppose any variations in the current through the circuit.
### Potential difference across an inductor in a circuit
![Pasted image 20260701145409](../assets/Pasted%20image%2020260701145409.png)
![Pasted image 20260701145424](../assets/Pasted%20image%2020260701145424.png)
$$V_{ab} = V_a - V_b = L\frac{di}{dt} \quad (30.8)$$

This equation calculates the potential difference (voltage) across the terminals of an inductor in a circuit.

- $V_{ab}$ is the potential difference between terminals $a$ and $b$.
- $L$ is the self-inductance of the component.
- $\frac{di}{dt}$ is the rate of change of the current over time.
    
While the induced electric field _inside_ the inductor's coils is non-conservative, the resulting charge separation at its terminals creates a genuine, conservative electrostatic potential difference. This distinction is what justifies treating the inductor as a standard circuit element with a definable voltage drop when applying Kirchhoff's loop rule.
